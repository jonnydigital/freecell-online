The opening in Klondike sets the ceiling for the rest of the game. If you reveal the wrong face-down cards first, or send cards to the foundation too early, you create constraints that compound as the game goes on. The first five moves are not just setup; they determine which sequences you can build and which columns have any chance of opening up.

Most players pick their first move based on what looks obvious. A better approach is to pick based on what creates the most useful state three moves later.

## Key Takeaways

- Revealing face-down cards is always the highest priority in the opening. More information means better decisions on every move that follows.
- Aces and 2s belong on the foundation immediately. No card benefits more from leaving the tableau than the space it frees up.
- Empty columns in Klondike should be earned, not created by moving a King speculatively. Wait until you have a King with a full sequence ready to follow.
- The column with the most face-down cards is usually the right target to dig into first. Flipping buried cards gives you options; shuffling face-up cards does not.
- After the first five moves, check whether you have more cards visible than you started. If not, reconsider your opening priority.

## What You Are Actually Trying to Do in the Opening

Klondike gives you seven tableau columns at the start. The leftmost has one card. The rightmost has seven, with six face-down. Between these extremes, every column has at least some hidden information.

Your opening job is to remove that hidden information as fast as possible. Revealed cards can be moved. Hidden cards are dead weight. The faster you flip, the more flexibility you build.

The secondary job is to send Aces and 2s to the foundation immediately. These cards have no useful tableau role. A 2 cannot build on anything in the tableau. Moving it to the foundation frees a space and unblocks whatever is beneath it.

The two priorities sometimes conflict. When they do, face-down cards usually win. The exception: if moving an Ace reveals a face-down card underneath it, that is two moves for the price of one.

## The Cards That Should Move First

Not all moves are equal in the opening. Here is the rough priority order:

Move an Ace to the foundation if one is available. Always. No exception.

Move a 2 to the foundation if its matching Ace is already there.

Move a face-up card that reveals a face-down card. The goal here is flipping, not building sequences. A move that reveals a new card is almost always better than one that only rearranges face-up cards.

Build in the tableau only when the build simultaneously reveals a face-down card, or when no revealing move is available.

Avoid moving cards between face-up columns without a clear purpose. These moves shuffle visible cards around without giving you new information.

## How to Handle Aces and 2s

The most consistent mistake in the Klondike opening is treating Aces and 2s as normal cards. They are not.

In the tableau, an Ace is essentially useless. Nothing builds on an Ace in Klondike. Keeping it in the tableau costs you a card slot without giving anything back. Move it to the foundation the moment you see it.

2s are similar. Once the matching Ace is on the foundation, a 2 should follow immediately. The 2 in the tableau blocks access to the card beneath it and does not serve as a build target, because a 3 can only go on the 2 after the 2 has been sent to the foundation.

The risk of holding Aces and 2s in the tableau is that they fill space that more useful cards could occupy. The [foundation timing guide](/blog/klondike-foundation-timing) explains which ranks are sometimes worth holding back, but Aces and 2s are never in that group.

## The King Decision: When to Open a Column

An empty column in Klondike creates a slot for a King. Kings open full sequences. But placing the wrong King wastes the slot.

The right time to place a King is when you have a known Queen to build beneath it. If you place a King and have no visible Queen that matches the required alternating color, you have filled the column with a King that cannot grow.

In the opening, empty columns are rare. When one appears, pause before filling it. Ask what you want to build there, not just which King is available. If you have a red King, a black Queen, and a red Jack all visible, that King is worth placing. If you have a King and nothing to follow it, the empty column may serve you better as a temporary buffer for rearranging other stacks.

## What Your Opening Reveals About the Current Game State

After five to seven moves, check these three things:

How many face-down cards did you flip? If the answer is fewer than three, your opening is probably too focused on rearranging face-up cards.

Are any Aces still sitting in the tableau without moving to the foundation? If yes, fix that on the next available turn.

Do you have more build targets than you had at the start? A good opening creates options: new face-up cards, more foundation cards, and at least one emerging sequence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I always move an Ace to the foundation immediately?

Yes. There is no situation in Klondike where keeping an Ace in the tableau helps you. An Ace in the tableau is occupying a card slot, blocking whatever is beneath it, and doing no useful work. Move it to the foundation the moment it is accessible.

### What is the best first move in Klondike?

The best first move reveals a face-down card. From the initial deal, flip the top cards of each face-down column and look for a card that allows you to move a face-up card from another column, uncovering a hidden one. If you can also move an Ace in the process, even better. The exact best first move depends on your specific deal, but "reveal the most face-down cards possible" is the consistent principle.

### How do I know if my opening is working?

A working opening means more information and more options after each move. If you flip three or four face-down cards in your first seven moves, you are on the right track. If you spend the opening moving face-up cards between columns without flipping anything new, the opening is stalling rather than building your position.

### Why does the opening matter so much in Klondike?

Because the opening sets the information state for everything that follows. Klondike's challenge is not just the rules; it is the hidden information. Every face-down card you cannot see is a variable you cannot plan around. The faster you clear that uncertainty in the opening, the better your decisions will be in the mid-game and endgame.
