Every Klondike deal starts with 21 face-down cards spread across seven columns. Your job for most of the game is to turn those cards over in the right order, fast enough that you run out of hidden cards before you run out of stock passes. How quickly you reveal them shapes every other decision you make.

## Key Takeaways

- Face-down card reveals are the primary progress signal in Klondike. Sequences and builds are the means, not the goal.
- The column with the most face-down cards usually deserves more attention than the one with the most visible face-up cards.
- Every move that does not reveal a face-down card is a move you spend without gaining new information. These moves need a clear reason.
- Stock cycling is what happens when your reveal rate stalls. Most unproductive passes trace to earlier moves that buried or blocked face-down cards.

## Why Face-Down Cards Are the Real Scoreboard

Most Klondike players track progress by foundation cards. A better scoreboard is remaining face-down cards. When that number drops, you are winning. When it stalls, you are in trouble.

A face-down card is a variable you cannot plan around. Until it flips, every sequence you build around it is provisional. If the hidden card is a 10 of Hearts and you need a 10 of Spades to free column 4, you will not know that until it shows. Players who reveal faster can plan better and recover from bad draws because they have more of the picture at any given moment.

The [Klondike opening moves guide](/blog/klondike-opening-moves) explains why the first five moves exist almost entirely to maximize early reveals. That principle does not stop at move five.

## The Column with the Most Buried Cards Needs Attention First

Every column in Klondike starts with a fixed number of face-down cards: column 1 has zero, column 2 has one, and column 7 has six. When you decide which face-up card to move, ask yourself which columns have the most buried cards and which move gets you one step closer to flipping one.

A move that clears the last face-up card from a deep column and triggers a reveal is usually worth more than a move that adds a card to an already-long sequence. The net gain in information is different, and information is what unlocks the next ten moves.

When two columns have similar reveal depth, prefer the one where the buried cards are most likely to be useful given the suits in play. If Hearts is already building well in the tableau, a flip in the column that has the most chance of showing a Heart card is slightly more valuable than flipping in a column where you can only guess.

## How to Maximize Reveals Per Turn

Not all reveal moves are equal. The most efficient reveals come from moves where one action clears a blocker and flips a card directly. The least efficient come from long chain builds where you move several cards to set up a sequence that eventually uncovers one card four or five moves later.

Before each move, place it in one of three categories: does this reveal a face-down card directly, does it set up a reveal on the next move, or does it do neither? Moves in the first category are the highest priority. Moves in the third category need a specific justification, like freeing a needed suit rank or protecting an open space.

Foundation plays are a partial exception. Sending an Ace or 2 to the foundation removes a low-rank card that would otherwise crowd the tableau without revealing anything immediately. The [foundation timing guide](/blog/klondike-foundation-timing) covers which ranks to prioritize and which ones are sometimes worth holding back.

## Why Blocked Face-Up Cards Cost More Than They Look

A face-up card that sits on top of a buried card and has no valid destination is a soft block. It is not dead, but it costs turns. Every stock pass that does not help move a soft block is turns spent cycling toward a worse position.

When you notice a face-up card that has no valid destination on the tableau and is not an Ace or 2, look ahead: what combination of future cards would create a landing spot for it? If the answer requires several specific stock cards appearing in order, treat that column as lower priority until conditions change.

## When Stock Cycles Mean Your Reveal Rate Has Stalled

Multiple stock cycles without new reveals are a signal, not just bad luck. They usually point to one of three causes: a column has been soft-blocked for several turns and you moved on without fixing it; foundation plays you avoided earlier are now needed to create landing spots; or an empty column you used for a speculative King has frozen a reveal path.

The [Klondike stock cycle trap guide](/blog/klondike-stock-cycle-trap) covers the specific move patterns that create cycle traps and how to recognize them early. The time to address a stalling reveal rate is the second unproductive pass, not the fourth.

You can [play Klondike free with undo](/klondike) and practice this deliberately: after each move, ask whether the face-down card count dropped. If not, ask whether the move set up a reveal on the next turn. Doing this consistently for a few sessions changes how you read a position.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many face-down cards does Klondike start with?

A standard Klondike deal has 21 face-down cards: column 1 has 0, column 2 has 1, column 3 has 2, and so on up to column 7 with 6. Clearing all 21 face-down cards before running out of stock passes is a strong indicator of a winnable position.

### Should I always take a reveal over any other move?

Most of the time, yes. The rare exception is when revealing a card in a short column would commit you to a sequence that locks out an open space you need. In practice, if you can flip a face-down card without losing a tableau space, you should do it.

### What do I do when no move reveals a face-down card?

First check whether a foundation play opens a new landing spot on the tableau. If nothing helps, draw from the stock. A stock card that creates a new build may allow a reveal on the next turn that was not possible before.

### How do I know which columns to prioritize?

Start with the deepest column by face-down card count. If two columns tie, prefer the one with face-up cards in suits you are actively building. The [Klondike opening strategy page](/klondike-opening-strategy) covers early column priorities, and the same logic extends through the mid-game.
