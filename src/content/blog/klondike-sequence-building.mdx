Long alternating-color sequences are the main tool you have in Klondike Solitaire. When you move a red 7 onto a black 8, you start a sequence. When that sequence grows to five or six cards, it becomes a lever: you can use it to uncover buried cards or earn an empty column. Most players build sequences reactively, placing whatever fits. This guide covers how to build them with intention.

## Key Takeaways

- The goal of a sequence is not length. It is the face-down card it will eventually reveal or the empty column it will free.
- Build sequences toward the longest buried stacks first. That is where the information you need is hiding.
- Mixed-color sequences are useful in the short term but expensive to dismantle. Do not rely on them for cards you plan to send to the foundation soon.
- Breaking a sequence is sometimes correct, but only when the card you are extracting has a direct purpose: revealing a buried card, sending an Ace or 2 to the foundation, or filling an empty column with a ready King.
- Every sequence you build has a hidden cost: the card that could have been placed somewhere else.

## What a Sequence Actually Does for You

A sequence in Klondike moves as a unit. If you have a black 9, red 8, black 7, and red 6 in order on the tableau, you can pick up all four and place them on a red 10. That mobility is the point.

Long sequences give you the ability to move groups of cards without having to break them apart first. They also tell you exactly where a card will land when you extend the sequence: the next card must be the opposite color and one rank lower.

The second thing sequences do is protect cards you are not yet ready to send to the foundation. A 5 sitting in a mid-sequence position is safe until the cards below it are gone. A 5 sitting alone on top of a face-down card is an obstacle with no immediate exit.

Play [Klondike free](/klondike) and look at your tableau after ten moves. The columns with the longest sequences are the ones you have the most leverage over.

## How to Decide Where to Build

At the start of a Klondike game, you have seven columns. Some have one face-up card. Some have six face-down cards under a single face-up one. Your sequence-building priority should almost always be: go toward the hidden cards.

A sequence on top of a short face-down stack will flip a buried card after one or two moves. A sequence on top of a tall face-down stack might take six moves to flip anything. Build toward tall stacks because each card you reveal there carries more value.

The [guide on which column to dig first](/blog/klondike-which-column-to-dig-first) explains this priority in detail. As a rule: each move you make should either reveal a face-down card, send a card to the foundation, or create the conditions for one of those things on the next move. Building a sequence for its own sake, without a clear payoff, is usually a tempo loss.

## Alternating Colors and Why the Rule Exists

Klondike requires alternating colors in a sequence: a black card must go on a red card, and a red card must go on a black card. The rule is not arbitrary. It prevents sequences from growing so long that they trap foundation-ready cards inside them.

In a correctly built sequence, every other card is the same color. When you send the lowest card in the sequence to the foundation, it moves cleanly because its neighbor is the opposite color and the sequence can be rebuilt without that card.

If you confuse suits with colors while building, you can end up with a card that looks like it fits but will block a future foundation move. The [Klondike cheat sheet](/klondike-cheat-sheet) has the color-rank matrix that prevents this mistake.

## When to Break a Sequence Apart

Breaking a sequence is sometimes the right move. These are the situations where it makes sense:

A face-down card is buried under a card in the middle of a sequence. If splitting the sequence lets you flip that card, the information gain is usually worth it, provided you have somewhere to put the top half.

A card deep in a sequence is needed immediately for the foundation. Aces and 2s should always be extracted no matter what it costs a sequence.

A King at the head of a sequence can fill an empty column, and the sequence has nowhere else useful to sit. Move the King and its followers into the empty column so other tableau cards can start building into the open space.

The situations where breaking a sequence is wrong: you have no destination for the top half, or the card you are trying to reach is not immediately actionable. Splitting a sequence just to see what is underneath it, without a concrete plan for the next two moves, usually makes the board worse.

## Mid-Game Sequence Pressure

In the mid-game, sequences start competing with each other for empty column space. You might have three partial sequences that each need an empty column to extend, but only one empty column exists.

The right choice is to prioritize the sequence that will produce the most new information. A sequence over a deep face-down stack wins over a sequence over a shallow stack or an already-visible card.

The secondary consideration is whether completing a sequence gets an Ace or 2 to the foundation. Foundation progress thins the tableau and reduces the number of cards you need to manage. See the [mid-game strategy guide](/blog/klondike-mid-game-strategy) for how sequence management fits into the broader tactical picture.

## Protecting Sequences You Have Already Built

Once a useful sequence exists, protect it from being split unnecessarily. A sequence sitting over a face-down card that you are about to flip should not be broken apart to serve another column unless the payoff is immediate.

The practical rule: if splitting a sequence would cost you more than two moves to reassemble, the extraction had better produce something worth two moves. Revealing a face-down card is worth it. Moving a card that could have waited is usually not.

This is the same logic behind [Klondike foundation timing](/blog/klondike-foundation-timing): the value of a move is not just what it does right now, but what it costs you in future options.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a Klondike tableau sequence be?

There is no target length. A sequence should be as long as it needs to be to reach the face-down card beneath it, or to fill an empty column with a ready King. Sequences that grow without a concrete goal are often trapping foundation cards that should be moving.

### Should I always build a sequence when I have a valid move?

No. A valid sequence move is not automatically a good move. Check first whether the move reveals a face-down card, sends a card to the foundation, or sets up one of those things on the next move. If the answer is none of the above, the move is shuffling face-up cards and costing you tempo.

### Can I build a sequence in the wrong direction?

Not in standard Klondike: cards must go lower in rank and alternate in color. The [Klondike opening strategy page](/klondike-opening-strategy) covers the exact rules for what can go where, which is helpful when you are scanning for valid sequence moves quickly.

### What happens if all my sequences are blocked?

If every sequence is blocked and the stock pile is exhausted, the game is likely over. Before that point, drawing from the stock and checking whether any draw extends a stuck sequence is the standard approach. The [Klondike complete guide](/blog/klondike-solitaire-complete-guide) covers what to do when the board tightens in the late game and sequences have no room to grow.
