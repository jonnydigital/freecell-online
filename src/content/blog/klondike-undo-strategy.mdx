Online Klondike gives you unlimited undo. Most players use it the same way: a move looks wrong, they hit undo, they try something else. That reflex is fine, but it's not strategy.

Knowing when to undo, when to hold back, and how to use undo deliberately separates reactive play from considered play. Here's how to think about it.

## Key Takeaways

- Undo removes your last move, but it doesn't change the underlying board problem that made the move look bad.
- In scored or timed Klondike, each undo usually costs points. Know the rules of your mode before relying on it.
- Reactive undo is fine for obvious errors. Deliberate undo is better for learning.
- Don't use undo to "unsee" a card you turned over. Even if you undo, remember what you saw.
- [Play Klondike Solitaire free](/klondike) to practice recognizing which moves deserve an undo and which don't.

## Reactive Undo vs Deliberate Undo

**Reactive undo** is what most players do. You make a move, immediately see it was wrong, and undo before doing anything else. It's a fast correction for clear mistakes: you moved a card to the wrong column, you sent an ace to the foundation too early, you made a play that exposed a card with nowhere useful to go.

Reactive undo is a valid tool. Use it. The question is whether that's all you're doing with it.

**Deliberate undo** is different. You make a move, play out several turns, then undo back to an earlier position to test a different path. You're running an experiment with the same deck, comparing where two different decisions lead.

This is more expensive in scored modes, but it teaches the game faster than replaying from scratch. You already know the card distribution. You can isolate the impact of one decision rather than playing a fresh random deal.

## What Undo Costs in Scored Mode

In standard timed Klondike, each undo usually deducts points from your score. The exact amount varies by game, but -2 per undo is common. In Vegas scoring, undo is typically disabled entirely because the mode is built around one-pass decisions.

If you're playing a scored mode:

- Reactive undo for a genuine blunder is usually worth the cost. One bad move undone is better than five moves recovering from it.
- Deliberate exploratory undo is expensive if you use it often. Save it for positions where the difference between two paths is significant.
- Undo addiction, where you undo any outcome that isn't perfect, kills your score faster than bad moves do.

Check your game's mode settings if you're not sure what undo costs. The [Klondike Vegas scoring page](/klondike-vegas-scoring) explains how strict scoring modes handle these decisions.

## When Reactive Undo Is Worth It

There are clear cases where hitting undo immediately is the right call:

**You moved a card to an obviously wrong column.** You put a red 6 on a black 7 when there was a better home for it. Before you draw another card, undo and reconsider.

**You sent a card to the foundation too early.** Foundation timing matters in Klondike. If you sent up a 6 and you can see that the 5 of the opposite color is buried, you may want it in the tableau a bit longer. The [Klondike foundation timing guide](/blog/klondike-foundation-timing) covers the specific cases where early foundation plays create problems downstream.

**You flipped a face-down card and the result is actively bad for your current plan.** You don't have to continue if the card you revealed blocks the next three moves you needed.

## When Reactive Undo Is Not Worth It

Some players undo almost any move that doesn't immediately pay off. That's a different problem.

**Don't undo because you didn't like what you turned over.** A bad reveal is information. If you undo and then make a different move before cycling back, you've changed the board state. The card you revealed is still under there. Use the information.

**Don't undo when you've drawn multiple stock cards since the move.** Once you've drawn two or three times after making a decision, you've changed what's accessible from the stock. Undoing back before those draws is usually more disruptive than it's worth unless the original move was genuinely catastrophic.

**Don't undo to avoid learning.** If every difficult board position gets reset rather than played through, you won't develop the skills to recognize when a position is actually stuck versus when it just looks bad. The [Klondike dead game detection guide](/blog/klondike-dead-game-detection) is worth reading alongside this one. Knowing when to resign is a different skill from knowing when to undo.

## Using Undo Deliberately

The best use of deliberate undo is the replay experiment: undo to a branch point and try the other option.

When this is useful:

- You're stuck 15 moves into a game and want to try a completely different approach to an early decision.
- You made a sequence of moves that felt logical but left you with a worse position than you expected. Undo back to before the sequence started.
- You dealt from the stock, didn't like the results, and want to try more tableau moves first before dealing again.

After each experiment, compare the results honestly. Don't just undo until you find an outcome you like. Ask which position is actually stronger and why.

The [Klondike mid-game strategy guide](/blog/klondike-mid-game-strategy) covers the types of decisions that create these branch points most often. Recognizing them is the first step to using deliberate undo well.

## The Information Undo Problem

When you flip a face-down card and immediately undo, you've seen what's there even if the board says you haven't. This creates a choice.

In casual play without scoring, using that information is fine. You turned over a 2, saw it was useless for the next ten moves, and undid to try a different path first. That's a reasonable learning move.

In strict or competitive modes, undo may not be allowed after a flip. In those cases, the face-down card you've committed to revealing is information you have to work with, not escape from.

The practical rule: whether you undo or not, remember what you saw. If you turn over a blocked 3 of hearts and undo, you now know that card is there. Use that when you're cycling the stock or planning excavation order. Pretending you didn't see it is a habit that makes you worse at the game, not better.

## Undo and Scoring Optimization

If you're trying to maximize your score rather than just finish the game, undo strategy shifts. Deliberate exploration becomes less useful because each undo costs points you're trying to protect. Reactive undo for clear errors stays worth it.

The best scored-mode approach is to be more deliberate before you move rather than more willing to undo after. Pause before each move. Ask whether there's a clearly better option. When there isn't, make the move and live with it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does undo cost points in Klondike Solitaire?

It depends on the mode. Standard timed Klondike usually deducts a fixed amount per undo. Vegas scoring typically disables undo entirely. Untimed or casual modes often allow unlimited undos with no penalty. Check your game settings if you're unsure.

### Can I undo as many times as I want?

In most online versions with no strict scoring, yes. Some competitive modes limit you to one undo per turn or disable it completely. Unlimited undo is common in relaxed play modes.

### Is using undo considered cheating?

No. It's a built-in feature. The only context where it matters is scored or competitive leaderboard play, where undo reduces your score or isn't available. In casual play, use it freely.

### Should I undo when I turn over a bad card from stock?

Only if a better tableau move was available before you drew. If you drew from the stock because no good move existed, the card you turned over is your new situation. Undoing and re-drawing won't change what's in the stock.

### How far back can I undo?

Most online versions let you undo one move at a time, as many times as you want. Some games let you step back several moves. There's usually no option to undo more than one move simultaneously, so a long deliberate experiment means multiple individual undos.
