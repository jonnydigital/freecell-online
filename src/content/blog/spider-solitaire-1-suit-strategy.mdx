Spider Solitaire's 1-suit mode uses 104 cards all from the same suit (typically spades), split across two decks. Every card can legally build on any card one rank higher, which removes the color-matching work that makes 2-suit and 4-suit hard. This is the mode where most players start, and for good reason: it teaches sequence building, empty column management, and deal timing without the constraint of tracking suit colors.

Starting in 1-suit does not make Spider easy. The board can still lock. Empty columns are still rare and expensive. The game rewards the same strategic thinking as the harder modes, just without the additional suit-matching layer.

## Key Takeaways

- In 1-suit Spider, any card builds on any card one rank higher. Use this to stack cards quickly and build long runs without worrying about color.
- Complete suits before dealing from the stock. Each deal buries your progress under ten new cards. Finish what you can before dealing again.
- Empty columns are your most valuable resource. Spend them on moves that directly advance a suit run or free a buried card.
- The endgame is simpler than 2-suit or 4-suit because you do not need to sort suits. Once you have a King-to-Ace run assembled, it is already complete.
- Winning consistently at 1-suit is the foundation for learning 2-suit. The column management skills transfer directly.

## How 1-Suit Mechanics Differ from Standard Spider

In 2-suit and 4-suit Spider, a stack of cards can only move as a group if every card in that stack shares the same suit. Mixed-suit stacks are legal but can only move one card at a time. This creates the main challenge of harder Spider modes: you can build long stacks, but they become immobile the moment they mix suits.

In 1-suit Spider, there is only one suit. Every card is the same suit by definition. This means every sequence you build is a proper in-suit sequence, and you can move the whole stack as a unit at any time.

The practical effect: you have far more flexibility in how you build. A 7 can go on any 8 without checking color or suit. You can consolidate scattered cards faster, and you can move multi-card groups freely.

What does not change: the King-to-Ace sequence still needs to be in proper descending order before it sends to the foundation. An 8 on a 7 is still an error, even in 1-suit.

## The Right Way to Build Sequences

Even though any card can go on any higher-ranking card in 1-suit mode, you should still build toward complete runs. A complete run goes from King down to Ace. Since all cards are the same suit, every card you play contributes to one of eight possible King-to-Ace sequences.

Avoid building short dead-end sequences. A stack that reads 9-8-7 sitting over a face-down 5 is blocking the 5 while contributing to a run in the wrong direction. Build toward filling in the gaps of your runs, not just stacking cards because they fit.

## Empty Columns: the Same Rule in Every Mode

Empty columns are just as scarce in 1-suit Spider as in harder modes. The game begins with no empty columns. You earn one by clearing all face-up cards from a column, which typically requires moving those cards onto other columns and flipping all the face-down cards underneath.

The rule for empty columns in 1-suit is the same as every Spider mode: spend them on moves that directly advance a complete run or flip a buried card. An empty column that absorbs a partial stack you wanted out of the way is usually a bad trade.

The best use of an empty column in 1-suit is as a routing slot. You move part of a run into the empty column, extract a card from underneath the remaining part, then reassemble the run. The column empties again after the reassembly and you can use it for the next routing move.

## When to Deal from the Stock

Spider gives you five deals during a game: ten cards per deal, one on each column. Each deal buries whatever is in your columns under a new card.

In 1-suit Spider, you have more control over your board than in harder modes because your sequences are fully mobile. Use that control to tidy columns before dealing.

Before each deal, try to:

- Complete any run within reach of the foundation. Each completion removes ten cards and frees a column.
- Clear at least one column entirely if possible. A deal into an empty column gives you an immediate free routing slot.
- Collapse scattered short stacks onto longer sequences. This reduces the number of columns that are blocking each other.

If your board has empty columns and no completed runs within reach, dealing is usually correct. If your board is cluttered with partial stacks and no empty columns, you may want to rearrange before dealing. Dealing into a cluttered board makes it harder, not easier.

## What to Do When the Board Gets Stuck

Even in 1-suit Spider, the board can tighten to a point where every move makes things worse. This usually happens when:

- All columns have face-down cards at the bottom and no empty columns exist for routing.
- Several partial runs are each missing one card, and that card is buried in the wrong column.
- The stock has been exhausted with no completed suits.

When stuck, look for the cheapest path to an empty column. Which column has the fewest face-down cards? Clear that one first. Once you have an empty column, more options open up.

## Moving to 2-Suit After 1-Suit

The biggest skill that 1-suit builds is reading your sequences and knowing which moves are reversible. In 2-suit mode, those same skills apply, but with the added rule that only same-suit sequences are fully mobile.

The adjustment to make: start tracking suit colors when you place cards. Every placement that mixes suits in a sequence costs you mobility later. The [2-suit strategy guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-2-suit-strategy) covers the exact habit changes to make when you step up from 1-suit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can any card stack on any other card in 1-suit Spider?

Any card can stack on any card that is exactly one rank higher. A 7 goes on an 8. A Queen goes on a King. Rank adjacency is still required; what relaxes is the suit-matching requirement. Since all cards are the same suit, every sequence you build is automatically a proper in-suit sequence.

### Why do I still lose in 1-suit Spider?

The most common 1-suit losses come from running out of empty columns before any suit is complete. This usually means the stock was dealt too early or partial stacks were not consolidated before dealing. The game plays like a puzzle: the moves are more flexible, but the column count is the same, and wasted columns still lock the board.

### Is 1-suit Spider easier than Klondike?

With optimal play, 1-suit Spider has a higher win rate than Klondike Solitaire. The main difference is that all cards are visible in Spider from the moment they are dealt face-up, while Klondike hides a large portion of the deck. More information means better decisions. The [Spider win rates guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-win-rates) has win-rate comparisons across Spider modes.

### How do I know when to deal in 1-suit Spider?

Deal when you have at least one empty column or when every column is blocked. Dealing with zero empty columns and a cluttered board usually makes things worse. If you can complete a suit or clear a column before dealing, do that first. A deal should feel like a reward for good play, not a way to escape a stuck position.
