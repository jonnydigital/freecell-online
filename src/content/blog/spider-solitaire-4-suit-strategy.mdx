In 1-suit Spider, every sequence move is valid and almost everything is reversible. In 4-suit Spider, nearly every move is a tradeoff. You can extend a sequence, but only by mixing suits, which costs you mobility later. You can deal new cards, but each deal buries whatever structure you built. The game is difficult not because the rules change but because the cost of every decision compounds across the board.

If you have been losing consistently on 4-suit, this guide will explain why.

## Key Takeaways

- Mixed-suit runs move one card at a time. In-suit runs move as a block. The difference becomes critical when you need to clear a column.
- Empty columns are rarer in 4-suit than in any other mode. Protect them and replace what fills them deliberately.
- The key tactical habit is restraint: skipping moves that look helpful but scatter your suits further.
- Choose one or two suits to build toward completion first. Spreading effort across all four suits at once rarely works.
- Deal timing matters more in 4-suit than in easier modes. The last two deals often decide the game.

## What Makes 4-Suit Harder Than You Expect

In 1-suit and 2-suit Spider, you can move entire runs freely because the suit colors match. In 4-suit, a run that mixes even two suits is stuck. The 8 of hearts sitting on the 9 of spades cannot move as a unit; you must move the 8 first, then the 9 separately.

This restriction turns long mixed runs into traps. A column that looks like five cards may actually be five individual cards, each needing its own empty landing spot to rearrange. And in 4-suit, those empty spots are precious.

The [Spider difficulty guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-difficulty-guide) compares all three modes and explains why 4-suit win rates drop so sharply. Most players find that 4-suit feels like a different game, not just a harder version of 2-suit. That feeling is accurate.

## The Mixed-Suit Run Problem

Every time you place a card on a run of a different suit, you create a lock. The card below it is reachable only if you can find somewhere to put the top card first, which requires either an empty column or another valid landing square.

The way this compounds: you make three or four mixed-suit moves early in the game, then realize midgame that the board has fractured into isolated single cards you cannot consolidate. The sequences are there visually, but they do not move as sequences.

The practical rule: before you make any move in 4-suit, ask whether the move is in-suit or mixed-suit. If it is mixed-suit, ask what you are gaining. A mixed-suit move is worth making when it flips a face-down card, opens an empty column, or extends a sequence you plan to complete soon. It is not worth making just to have a legal move.

The [Spider suit strategy guide](/spider-suit-strategy) has a full breakdown of when mixed-suit plays are worth the cost. In 4-suit, that guide applies at almost every turn.

## Empty Column Economics in 4-Suit

An empty column in Spider is worth more than any other asset on the board. It can hold any card, any run, any sequence temporarily. In 4-suit, empty columns are also much harder to create and much easier to waste.

Creating an empty column in 4-suit usually requires either:

- Moving a short suited run off the top of a column, which requires another column to land on
- Cycling through the stock until a helpful card appears for a nearly empty column

Once you have an empty column, use it purposefully. The options are:

- Park a key card there while you rearrange a sequence
- Move a mixed run in temporarily to clear access to a buried card
- Let the deal fill it when no better option exists (rare and reluctant)

The [empty columns deep-dive](/blog/spider-solitaire-empty-columns) applies to all difficulties, but the underlying logic is sharpest in 4-suit, where the gap between using a column well and wasting it on the nearest card is usually ten or fifteen turns of downstream consequence.

One principle specific to 4-suit: never fill an empty column with a King unless that King can attract a Queen you already have. A lone King with no accessible followers is just an expensive parking spot.

## Picking Which Suit to Build First

You have four suits and one board. Trying to advance all four simultaneously spreads your empty columns and suited runs too thin. Most winning 4-suit games are built on one or two suits getting completed early, which frees up columns for the rest.

How to pick the lead suit:

1. Look at which suit has the longest unbroken in-suit run already. That suit has head start momentum.
2. Look at which suit's high cards (Ace through 5 or 6) are already face-up in accessible positions.
3. Look at which suit is buried least. If all four of a suit's Kings are under face-down cards, that suit will be expensive to complete.

Once you pick a lead suit, make its in-suit sequences a priority over other moves. Accept some mixed-suit pileup in the other suits if it keeps your lead suit clean.

The [suit completion guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-suit-completion) explains how to recognize when a suit can be completed in the next few moves. In 4-suit, that recognition window is smaller and harder to spot.

## Pre-Deal Discipline at the Hardest Level

The [when to deal guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-when-to-deal) lays out the standard pre-deal checklist. In 4-suit, every item on that checklist is more consequential.

The five questions to answer before dealing in 4-suit:

1. Have you made every card-flipping move? Flips are harder to set up in 4-suit, so each one is more valuable.
2. Have you consolidated any in-suit runs that can be combined? Once the new cards land, consolidation opportunities close quickly.
3. Is every empty column filled with a card that earns its space? In 4-suit you cannot afford a column holding a card that goes nowhere.
4. Have you counted the remaining deals? The stock holds five deals of ten. If you are two deals in with no complete suits, the board is behind pace.
5. Is there any move you can make that will immediately pay off in the next few turns? If not, dealing is probably correct.

The count matters especially in 4-suit. By the time the stock is empty, you should have at least one or two complete suits already removed. If you are dealing the fifth round with the board still fragmented across four suits, the game is nearly always lost.

## Endgame Patterns That Win

In a winning 4-suit game, the endgame looks like this: two or three suits already removed, the remaining board concentrated into a few well-organized columns, and the last deal bringing cards that can be slotted directly onto existing sequences.

How to work toward that pattern:

- Prioritize suit completion over board tidiness. A messy board with two completed suits is better than a tidy board with zero.
- Use your late empty columns to sequence the final runs, not to hold stray cards.
- Once a suit is within five cards of completion, clear other moves first and complete it cleanly. Leaving a near-complete suit fragmented for several turns is a common way to lose winnable positions.
- When the stock is empty, assess clearly. If you have completed two or three suits and the remaining columns have visible in-suit structure, you are probably in a winnable position. If you have completed zero suits and the board is fragmented, accept the loss and deal again.

The [Spider endgame strategy page](/spider-endgame-strategy) covers the final board logic in full. In 4-suit, the same principles apply but the margin is tighter: one misplaced card in the final sequences can make an otherwise completeable board unresolvable.

[Play Spider Solitaire](/spider) and start with 1-suit to build the deal-timing instinct, then 2-suit for mixed run practice, then bring both skills into 4-suit. The transition will feel hard at first. It gets easier once you stop thinking about moves and start thinking about suits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a realistic win rate for 4-suit Spider?

For most players, 4-suit win rates land in the range of 10 to 30 percent with unrestricted undo. Without undo, winning consistently requires strong board reading and deal discipline. Professional-level play can push the win rate higher, but the game has an inherent randomness floor where some deals are not solvable regardless of skill.

### Should I use undo frequently in 4-suit?

Yes, especially while learning. Undo lets you try a sequence, observe the consequence, and understand why it failed. In 4-suit, the cost of a wrong move is often invisible for ten or fifteen turns, and undo is the only way to trace it back. As your reading improves, you will undo less because you will see the consequences before committing.

### Is it ever worth breaking up an in-suit run to enable another move?

Rarely. Breaking an in-suit run creates two partial sequences where you had one mobile one. The only time it is defensible is when the run needs to be broken to complete a different suit that is one or two cards from removal. Even then, have a plan to rebuild it before you commit.

### When should I give up on a 4-suit game?

When the last deal has landed, no suits are complete, and the board has no consolidatable sequences, the game is almost certainly lost. If you have been using undo and cannot find a path forward, the position is either genuinely unwinnable or would require too many moves to find without a solver. The [Spider winnability page](/spider-winnability) covers how often 4-suit games are solvable in principle.

### How is 4-suit different from 1-suit besides being harder?

The structural difference is mobility. In 1-suit, every run moves as a unit because all cards share one suit. In 4-suit, almost nothing moves as a unit unless you built it in-suit deliberately. That means 4-suit requires active construction of mobile sequences, not just sequencing of the cards the game gives you. The [Spider cheat sheet](/spider-cheat-sheet) has a quick reference on when runs are mobile and when they are locked.
