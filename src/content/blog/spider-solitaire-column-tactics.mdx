In Spider Solitaire, the ten columns are not equal. Some will become empty during the game. Some will bury face-down cards for many turns. Some will quietly become problem columns that block everything around them. How you manage each column individually is what separates a game that opens up from one that locks.

Most Spider strategy advice covers the big picture: build in-suit runs, protect empty columns, be careful when dealing. Column tactics go one level deeper. They answer a more specific question: given the current state of this column, what is the right thing to do with it?

## Key Takeaways

- Each column has a different value depending on its face-down count, its current face-up arrangement, and the stage of the game.
- Columns with the fewest face-down cards are the fastest paths to an empty column. Prioritize clearing them.
- A column with a long mixed-suit face-up stack is expensive to fix. Avoid adding cards to it unless you are routing through it briefly.
- Sacrificing a column is sometimes the right call, but only when it immediately frees something more valuable.
- Column management thinking becomes most important in the mid-game when deals are limited and every column affects every other.

## How to Read Each Column's State

Before you move any cards, scan all ten columns and get a rough sense of what each one is doing for you.

An active column has face-down cards at the bottom and is worth digging. The goal for these columns is to flip face-down cards as quickly as possible.

A useful stack is a column with a workable face-up arrangement, ideally in-suit. These are your building blocks. Try to extend them rather than disrupt them.

A routing column is one you are using temporarily to move cards around before reassembling them elsewhere. These should return to their previous state, or better, as quickly as possible.

A garbage column has a mixed-suit face-up stack that cannot move as a unit. In 2-suit and 4-suit Spider, a mixed-suit stack can only be moved one card at a time. These columns are expensive to fix and easy to make worse.

An empty column is the most valuable resource in Spider. Spend it carefully and always plan how to recover it.

## Which Column to Dig First

Spider deals start with face-down cards under each column's top card. In a standard 104-card deal, columns have varying depths of face-down cards depending on the deal.

The fastest path to your first empty column is almost always the column with the fewest face-down cards. A column with one face-down card can become empty in just a few moves. A column with five face-down cards requires significantly more work and more routing space to clear.

Dig the shallowest column first. Every flip adds new information and moves you closer to the empty column that unlocks more options.

The exception is when a different column's top card is needed immediately to extend a near-complete suit run. If extending that run would complete a suit and free a column on its own, that takes priority. But as a default early-game rule, dig from the shortest column first.

## The Routing Column Pattern

The routing column pattern is the most common column tactic in mid-game Spider. You move part of a run into an empty column, extract the card you need from another column, then reassemble the run. The empty column returns to empty after the operation.

The critical rule: if you end the operation with cards still sitting in the routing column, you have lost an empty column for no gain. That is one of the most common ways good games collapse.

Before using an empty column as a routing column, plan the full sequence of moves including the reassembly step. If you cannot see all the way to the reassembly, you are likely about to strand cards there.

## Garbage Columns and How They Form

A garbage column builds up when you place cards from different suits in descending order without planning how to move them later. In 2-suit and 4-suit Spider, only same-suit sequences can move as a unit. A stack of 8-7-6 where the 8 and 6 are spades but the 7 is hearts looks valid but cannot move as a group. Fixing it requires routing each card individually, which burns routing columns and empty columns in the process.

Avoid adding cards to a garbage column unless you are routing through it temporarily with a plan to extract those cards within three moves. The [Spider suit strategy page](/spider-suit-strategy) explains how to track suit identity in your stacks and why same-suit sequences are worth protecting even when a mixed build is available.

## When to Sacrifice a Column

Sometimes you have to accept a bad card into a relatively clean column when it has nowhere useful to go. Sacrificing a column can be correct: dealing buries your progress under ten new cards, while a sacrificed column can often be fixed with routing once you have more space.

The key condition: you need a plan for fixing the column within the next several moves. Sacrificing without a recovery path turns it into a garbage column. Think at least three moves ahead before placing a card into a column you want to protect. Burning an empty column with no extraction plan is rarely worth it.

## Column Count as a Pacing Signal

Spider's board is ten columns wide. Each completed suit removes ten cards. Eight suits empty the board.

Think of the number of healthy columns as a pacing signal. At the start, all ten columns are active. As suits complete, the board gets simpler. As columns fill with garbage or get buried under deals, the board gets harder.

Your goal is to complete suits before the board degrades. Too many deals without suit completions usually means the board will lock before the final few suits can be completed.

After each deal, check how many columns are in a good state. A rough rule: if more than half are either clean or actively progressing, you are on track. If more than half are stalled or garbage, the next deal is likely to make things worse rather than better.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many empty columns do I need to play Spider effectively?

One empty column changes the game considerably because it enables the routing pattern. Two empty columns allow complex multi-step operations. Three or more usually means you are in strong shape. The number of empty columns you are able to maintain is the clearest signal of how well your column tactics are working.

### Should I always dig the shallowest column first in Spider?

Almost always in the opening. The exception is when a different column's top card is needed immediately to extend a near-complete suit run that would free a column on its own. But as a default rule, shorter columns become empty faster, and empty columns win games.

### What makes a column turn into a garbage column?

Mixed-suit stacking is the main cause. In 2-suit and 4-suit Spider, placing a hearts card on a spades card in the same stack creates a stack that can only move one card at a time. The problem does not show up immediately. It appears later when you need to route that stack and discover it takes five individual moves instead of one group move. Tracking suit identity when you build prevents this.

### When is it okay to use an empty column as temporary storage?

When you have a clear plan to move the card out within two or three moves. An empty column used as a genuine temporary stop, with a specific recovery move already identified, is just routing. An empty column used as permanent storage for a card with nowhere else to go is a lost column. The difference is whether you know how you are getting the card back out before you put it in.
