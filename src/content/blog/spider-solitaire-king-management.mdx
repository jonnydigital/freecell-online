Every card in Spider Solitaire can eventually move somewhere. Except the king. Once a king is on the tableau, nothing goes above it. It can only sit at the base of a stack, either as the anchor of a sequence you are building or as a problem you are working around.

Most Spider strategy focuses on suits, empty columns, and deal timing. King management gets less attention, but it shapes those other decisions. A king in the wrong column at the wrong time can make an otherwise playable board very hard to finish.

## Key Takeaways

- Kings cannot be placed on any other card in Spider Solitaire, making them permanent column anchors.
- Never fill an empty column with a king unless you have a specific plan for building a sequence from it soon.
- A king placed without a plan costs you a high-value resource: the empty column itself.
- In 4-suit Spider, king placement is even more critical because in-suit sequences take longer to build.
- If a king is already in a difficult position, focus on building around it rather than waiting for a way out.

## Why Kings Are Different from Every Other Card

In Spider, you can build sequences down in rank regardless of suit. A 9 of any suit can sit on a 10 of any suit. That flexibility is what makes the game playable.

Kings break this rule. There is nothing above a 13, so no card can accept a king. A king that lands in a column stays there until the entire sequence under it is complete and sent to the foundation. That can be dozens of moves away, or it can be never.

This makes kings valuable in one specific way: they are the only card that can anchor a long sequence without any risk of being needed elsewhere. A king at the base of a complete 13-card in-suit sequence goes directly to the foundation. That is the best outcome.

The dangerous scenario is the opposite: a king that lands in an empty column with no plan behind it. You have used a high-value resource (an empty column) to park a card with no useful moves available. ## The Empty Column King Trap

The most common king mistake in Spider is this: a column empties out, and the next available card to drop into it is a king. It feels natural to fill the space.

The problem is that placing a lone king in an empty column without a plan is almost always wrong. Here is why:

You needed that empty column to move sequences around and flip face-down cards. The king you placed there is now frozen until you build a complete sequence beneath it. Building that sequence will likely require using other empty columns. And those empty columns are now gone too.

An empty column in Spider is one of the most valuable resources in the game. Using it to park a lone king gives you nothing immediate in return. If you do not have a clear line from that king to a completed suit, you have traded your best tool for a placeholder.

The exception: if you have a nearly complete sequence that just needs a king as its base, placing the king first and then building down quickly is a legitimate plan. "Quickly" means you have the cards ready and accessible right now, not several deals away.

## How to Use a King as a Column Anchor

A king earns its column when you have a plan. Specifically, place a king when:

- You already have the cards from a queen down to the 2 and ace of the matching suit ready to build.
- You are playing 1-suit Spider and can build in-suit from almost any position.
- You have a partial sequence that needs the king to formalize the base, and you can reach the remaining cards without needing the empty column for something else first.

In these cases, the king accelerates a suit completion rather than blocking one. Place the king, build the sequence, and clear the suit.

Even in these situations, check the board before committing. Does placing this king take an empty column out of circulation for an extended stretch? If you will need that space for other maneuvering before you can finish the sequence, delay the king and use the empty column for something more immediate.

## Reading Your Board Before Placing a King

Before placing any king in an empty column, run through this check:

**How many empty columns do you have?** If you are down to one or two, placing a king in one of them is a high-cost move. You will feel that shortage almost immediately.

**Is the sequence buildable from the current board state?** Look for the queen that would go below the king. Can you reach it without needing more empty space? Then check the jack, ten, and so on down to the ace. If the sequence breaks anywhere because key cards are buried, wait.

**What phase of the game is this?** Early in the game, before the first deal, you rarely want to place a king in an empty column. The board is too chaotic and you need flexibility. Later in the game, after several deals, kings become easier to commit to because you can see more of the remaining deck.

## What to Do When a King Is Already Blocking You

Sometimes the king is already in a bad position. Maybe it appeared in a deal, or a sequence you were building collapsed. Now you have a column that is difficult to clear.

Here is how to work around it:

**Build beneath it anyway, even out of suit.** A king with a long face-up stack on top is still useful as a staging area. You can route sequences through it and keep the column active for maneuvering.

**Use it as a temporary parking spot.** An out-of-suit sequence parked under a king is not ideal, but it is better than having those cards scattered across other columns where they block face-down cards. You can move the sequence off later when a better column opens.

**Accept the constraint.** Sometimes a blocked column is just a constraint you play around for the rest of the game. Focus on the other columns. Try to complete a suit elsewhere, which clears cards and may open new options. The [Spider endgame strategy guide](/spider-endgame-strategy) covers how to convert tight positions into wins.

## King Placement in 4-Suit Spider

In 4-suit Spider, in-suit sequences are much harder to build. A king placed in an empty column usually represents a longer commitment than in 1-suit. You are rarely going to drop a complete in-suit sequence beneath it in the near term.

Because of this, treat kings with extra caution in 4-suit games. Avoid placing them in empty columns unless you are very close to completing a specific suit and the sequence path is clear. A king placed too early in a 4-suit game can be an anchor you are managing around for the rest of the session.

## Practice King Awareness

King management becomes more intuitive after a few dozen games. The main habit to build is pausing before placing any king in an empty column and asking: do I have a clear, near-term path from this king to a completed suit?

If the answer is no, hold off. The king will still be there when you are ready. Use the empty space for something that creates options now.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a king ever be moved in Spider Solitaire?

Yes. A king can move to any empty column. That is its only legal destination. It cannot be placed on top of any other card.

### Should I always fill an empty column with a king?

No. An empty column is often more useful as a maneuvering space than as a king anchor. Only place a king in an empty column if you have a clear plan to build a sequence from it soon.

### What happens if multiple kings end up blocking different columns?

This is one of the harder positions to recover from in Spider. Prioritize completing any partial sequences that are visible, use any open spaces to reorder face-up cards, and focus on freeing at least one empty column. If three or more columns are king-anchored with no clear sequence plan under any of them, the game becomes very difficult to win.

### Is king management different in 1-suit vs 4-suit Spider?

Significantly. In 1-suit, building a full sequence beneath a king is achievable quickly because every card shares the same suit. In 4-suit, building in-suit is far slower, so king placement carries more long-term risk. Be much more selective about placing kings in empty columns when playing 4-suit.
