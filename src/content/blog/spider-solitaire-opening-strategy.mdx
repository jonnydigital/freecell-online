The Spider Solitaire opening is the phase nobody talks about. Before the first deal hits, you have 10 face-up cards across 10 columns and somewhere between 10 and 25 moves available depending on the board. Those moves decide whether you enter the first deal with structure or with a mess.

Most players treat the opening as warmup and deal as soon as they run out of obvious moves. Strong players treat the opening as the most important phase in the game, because it is the only phase where you have full control without the chaos of incoming cards.

## Key Takeaways

- The opening phase covers all moves before the first stock deal. Getting it right means entering the deal with at least one near-complete suited run and at least one column with few cards remaining.
- Prioritize same-suit connections over rank-only connections. A 7 placed on an 8 of the same suit is worth twice what a 7 on an 8 of a different suit achieves.
- Empty columns do not appear in the opening through luck. They require deliberate consolidation of short columns.
- Do not rush. The opening deals no time pressure. Every move should have a clear purpose.
- The full [Spider opening strategy page](/spider-opening-strategy) covers the complete framework. This post focuses on the habits that make those principles work move by move.

## What the Opening Phase Actually Is

In Spider Solitaire, the stock holds 50 cards divided into five deals of 10. The first deal places 10 new cards on the board, one per column.

The opening phase is everything before that first deal: the moves you make on the initial layout of face-up cards that came out at the start.

On a standard board, the opening shows 10 columns. Each column has one face-up card visible, with four to five face-down cards beneath it. The opening is the period where you work those initial face-up cards into sequences before the first deal buries them under new cards.

The opening is short in time but high in impact. Bad opening habits send you into the first deal with blocked columns, no suited runs started, and no path toward an empty column. Good habits build a foundation that compounds across every deal that follows.

## First Priority: Same-Suit Connections

Every move you make in Spider puts a card on top of another card. The only moves that build long-term structure are same-suit connections.

A 7 placed on an 8 of a different suit creates a tableau stack. It helps temporarily with rank sequencing but the two-card group cannot be moved as a unit, and it contributes nothing toward suit completion. A 7 placed on an 8 of the same suit creates a suited run that moves as a block and counts directly toward the 13-card completion condition.

In [1-suit Spider](/spider/1-suit-vs-2-suit-vs-4-suit), every connection is same-suit by definition, so this is automatic. In 2-suit and 4-suit games, you have a choice on most early moves, and the wrong choice creates mixed runs that fragment your board for the rest of the game.

The rule: when a same-suit connection is available, take it even if a rank-only connection is closer or would uncover a card faster. Same-suit structure compounds. Mixed-suit structure dead-ends. The [suit strategy guide](/spider-suit-strategy) covers this principle across the whole game. In the opening, the application is simple: sort by suit first, rank second.

## Second Priority: Uncover Face-Down Cards

The opening phase has face-down cards in every column. You cannot plan around a card you cannot see.

After taking any available same-suit connections, your second goal is to flip as many face-down cards as possible. Moving a face-up card onto another column exposes the card beneath it. That flip may reveal something useful or neutral, but it always reduces the hidden information on the board.

In practice: if you have a rank connection available that does not create a same-suit run, weigh it against whether the move will expose a face-down card. A move that flips a face-down card is almost always worth making even when the suit does not match. The information gain is that valuable in the opening, because every flip either gives you a new option or confirms a path you already planned.

## Third Priority: Work Toward an Empty Column

An empty column is your most powerful tool in Spider. It appears when you move all face-up cards from a column. In most opening boards, you cannot reach an empty column by the time the first deal arrives. But you can set up columns that are one or two moves from empty before the deal lands.

That matters because the deal fills every column, including empty ones. A column with only one face-up card before the deal becomes a column with two face-up cards after the deal. A column with four face-up cards before the deal becomes a column with five. Shorter columns before the deal means shorter columns after it.

Look at the columns with the fewest face-up cards in the opening. If moving their face-up cards elsewhere creates a same-suit connection or exposes a face-down card, make those moves. A near-empty column going into the first deal is a strategic asset you will use throughout the game.

The [empty columns guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-empty-columns) covers the full management strategy across all five deals. In the opening, the goal is simpler: identify the column with the fewest face-up cards and consolidate toward it.

## What Not to Do in the Opening

**Do not scatter cards randomly just to flip face-down cards.** Flipping is good, but flipping by making cross-suit connections on your shortest columns destroys their potential to become near-empty before the first deal.

**Do not build long mixed-suit runs.** Building a 6-card tableau sequence in the opening feels productive. If it is all mixed suits, you have used those moves to create a sequence that cannot move as a group and counts for nothing toward completion. The [how to empty a column guide](/spider/how-to-empty-a-column) explains why mixed runs clog your pathways when you need them most.

**Do not move Kings unnecessarily.** Kings are anchors for future empty columns. Moving a King from one column to another uses a move and fills a spot on the destination column without real gain. Leave Kings in place unless they are sitting on a face-down card you need to flip.

## Reading the Opening Board

Before making your first move, scan all 10 columns. Note three things:

1. Which face-up cards can connect same-suit.
2. Which columns have only one face-up card (the candidates for near-empty columns).
3. Which columns have Kings on top (leave them alone unless a face-down flip depends on it).

This scan takes about 10 seconds. It turns a reactive opening into a sequence of purposeful moves. The [board reading guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-position-reading) applies this four-point scan to mid-game positions. The same thinking applies in the opening, just with a shorter list of visible cards.

After the opening, check the [pre-deal checklist](/blog/spider-solitaire-when-to-deal) before clicking the stock. The opening ends when your checklist comes up empty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many moves should I aim for in the Spider opening?

There is no fixed target. Make every move that creates a same-suit connection or uncovers a face-down card. Stop when no productive moves remain and run the pre-deal checklist. Forcing extra moves just to stay in the opening phase does more harm than good.

### Should I focus on one suit in the opening?

In 2-suit and 4-suit games, yes. Identify which suit has the most visible face-up cards and prioritize same-suit connections for that suit. Early concentration in one suit pays off faster than spreading effort across all suits simultaneously.

### Is the opening different for 1-suit vs 4-suit Spider?

In 1-suit Spider, every move is same-suit by definition, so suit tracking does not apply. The focus is entirely on flipping face-down cards and working toward a near-empty column. In 4-suit Spider, suit management in the opening matters more than in any other phase, because early cross-suit connections create locked sequences that take many later moves to correct.

### What if my opening board has no same-suit connections?

Make the moves that expose face-down cards and set up near-empty columns, even if everything is cross-suit. The opening board is random. Some boards give you eight same-suit connections to make in the opening. Some give you two. Work with what you have and deal when your checklist clears.

### How do I practice opening strategy on its own?

Start a 2-suit game and play only the opening phase. Before dealing, count how many face-down cards you flipped, how many same-suit runs you built, and how close your shortest column is to empty. Restart and try to improve each number. After ten sessions of this focused practice, the opening scan becomes automatic and you stop wasting moves on low-value cross-suit placements.
