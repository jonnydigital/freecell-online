Moving a sequence in Spider Solitaire looks like progress. You shift cards, combine columns, and sometimes open space underneath. But not every sequence move advances the position. Some just relocate a problem from one part of the board to another, leaving you with the same constraints a few moves later and fewer options to work with.

Knowing when to move a sequence and when to hold it is one of the clearest differences between players who win consistently and players who stay stuck.

## Key Takeaways

- A sequence move that does not reveal a buried card or free a column is usually neutral at best. Do not make it just because it fits.
- Moving a mixed-suit sequence onto a card of a different suit blocks future in-suit run building in that area. Only accept that trade when the gain elsewhere is specific and clear.
- The best sequence moves accomplish two things at once: they place the sequence on a useful receiving card AND reveal something useful underneath.
- Empty columns are the most flexible tool for routing sequences. Spending one to move a sequence you did not need to move is expensive.
- You can [play Spider Solitaire free](/spider) and use undo on any sequence move to check whether the result actually improved the board.

## What Moving a Sequence Actually Costs

In Spider Solitaire, moving a sequence always has a destination cost and sometimes a column cost. If a valid receiving card of the right rank exists on the tableau, you can move the sequence there directly. If not, you need an empty column to act as a routing space.

Spending an empty column is not free. Empty columns are Spider's most powerful resource. Once you use one as a relay for a sequence, it is gone until you clear another column from scratch. Before spending one, ask: is the resulting position meaningfully better, or just rearranged? The [Spider Solitaire empty columns guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-empty-columns) covers how to value and protect open columns throughout the game.

Even when a valid receiving card exists, the destination suit matters. Placing a Hearts sequence onto a Spades card creates a mixed-suit sequence that cannot be removed as a complete suit run. You are locking in incompatibility. That cost is often invisible right after the move and becomes a serious problem when you are trying to complete suits in the endgame.

## When a Sequence Move Creates Real Progress

Sequence moves are worth making when they accomplish at least one of three things:

**Reveal a buried card.** The most valuable sequence moves clear enough face-up cards to flip one that was hidden underneath. This gives you new information and new options. Most of the high-value moves in Spider follow this pattern.

**Free a column.** Moving a sequence out of a column entirely, leaving it empty, creates one of Spider's most powerful resources. This is worth spending significant effort to achieve, including using an existing empty column as a relay.

**Combine partial runs of the same suit.** If your Hearts 9-8-7 is on one column and a Hearts 10 is on another, merging them into a longer Hearts run is direct progress toward suit completion. The [Spider suit strategy guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-suit-strategy) covers how to recognize and pursue these combinations throughout the game.

If a sequence move does not do at least one of these three things, slow down and look again.

## The In-Suit Test Before You Move

Before placing any sequence, run a quick check: does this placement make a future in-suit run harder to form?

If you stack a Hearts sequence on a Spades card, that Hearts sequence is now committed to a location that does not match its suit base. To continue building the in-suit Hearts run later, you will need to move the entire mixed-suit group somewhere else first. That is extra work you are creating.

The test is simple. If the destination card is the same suit as the top card of the sequence you are placing onto it, the move preserves in-suit potential. If it is a different suit, you are accepting incompatibility. Sometimes that is the right call, but it should be a conscious decision, not a reflex.

## Using Empty Columns to Route Without Committing

An empty column lets you pick up a sequence, place it temporarily, and then route it to its real destination once the path clears. This is the most flexible way to move sequences when no direct receiving card is available.

The risk is treating the empty column as a permanent home. Players will sometimes move a sequence into an empty space and then start building on it, which makes it harder to move later and removes the column's value as open space. Use empty columns as routing positions, not target columns, unless the sequence you place there is ready to stay and grow into a suit run.

The [Spider column tactics guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-column-tactics) covers column management in depth. Sequence routing is a specific case of that broader discipline, and the same principles apply.

## When No Sequence Move Is Right

Some positions have no good sequence move. Every option either burns an empty column for a neutral result, creates a mixed-suit dead end, or reveals a card that does not help your current priorities. This is the moment to consider dealing from the stock.

Dealing is not giving up. It adds new cards to work with and sometimes breaks the logjam by creating receiving opportunities that did not exist before. The [Spider Solitaire when-to-deal guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-when-to-deal) covers the signals that tell you dealing is better than another sequence move right now.

If you keep landing in positions with no good sequence moves, review the last few turns. A move that looked neutral earlier probably created the constraint you are now dealing with.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I move a mixed-suit sequence in Spider Solitaire?

Yes. Any group of cards in descending rank can be moved as a unit, regardless of suit. The trade-off is that a mixed-suit sequence cannot be completed as a full suit run. Only move mixed-suit sequences when the reason to do so is clear, not because the move is available.

### Does moving a sequence always use up an empty column?

Only when no valid receiving card of the right rank exists on the tableau. If a valid receiving card is there, you can place the sequence directly without using an empty column as a relay.

### How do I know if a sequence move was a mistake?

A useful check: three or four moves after the sequence move, is the board easier to work with? If you dealt from the stock sooner than expected, ran short on valid plays, or burned an empty column for a result you cannot name, the earlier sequence move was probably neutral or worse.

### What is the difference between moving a long sequence versus a short one?

Moving a longer sequence commits more cards to a new location at once, which reduces your flexibility. Before moving a sequence of five or more cards, ask whether you could achieve the same result by moving fewer cards and leaving the rest in place. Shorter moves often create the same opening without the same commitment cost.
