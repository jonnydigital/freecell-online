In 1-suit Spider, every card fits every sequence, so tracking suits is not relevant. In 2-suit and 4-suit Spider, it matters a lot. Knowing how many cards of each suit are still face-down changes which in-suit runs are worth committing to and which are likely to stall because the suit is scattered across buried columns.

This is what suit tracking means: watching the visible suit distribution as cards are revealed and using that information to decide which runs to build.

## Key Takeaways

- Spider Solitaire uses two full 52-card decks, giving each suit exactly 26 cards. At any point in a 4-suit game, you can count how many are exposed versus hidden.
- A suit with many cards still face-down is high risk to build toward early. A suit with many cards already visible is a better target.
- Suit tracking is not memorization. It is a rough count: more than half revealed is favorable, fewer than a quarter exposed is a warning sign.
- Use suit tracking to decide between in-suit and cross-suit building when you have both options.
- [Play Spider Solitaire free](/spider) in 2-suit or 4-suit mode and practice counting exposed cards per suit after the first few moves.

## The Starting Numbers

Spider Solitaire uses 104 cards: two full decks. For a 4-suit game, that means 26 cards per suit (13 ranks, 2 copies each). For a 2-suit game, it means 52 cards per suit.

At the start of a 4-suit game, the board shows only the top card of each column: 10 cards visible. Those 10 exposed cards come from all four suits, but unevenly. Some suits might have two or three cards visible. Others might have none at all.

The remaining 94 cards are either face-down in the columns or sitting in the stock. You have almost no information about which suits are accessible. That is the baseline you work from.

The [Spider Solitaire position reading guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-position-reading) covers the four things worth assessing before each move. Suit tracking is a refinement of one of those elements: knowing not just how many face-down cards exist but which suits those hidden cards belong to.

## Why Suit Distribution Matters

In 4-suit Spider, a sequence only moves as a complete unit if it is pure: every card the same suit. A mixed-suit sequence is legal to move, but it will never complete as a full suit run, and moving it elsewhere costs moves and can block future in-suit building.

Before you commit to building an in-suit run, ask: are enough cards of this suit visible to make the run achievable?

If 20 of the 26 Spades are still buried across face-down cards and the stock, chasing a Spades run in the opening rounds is likely to stall. You will build partway, run out of Spades within reach, and have committed column space and moves to a run that cannot yet complete.

Contrast that with a suit where 15 or more cards are already exposed across the tableau. The cards exist within reach. Building toward a complete run is realistic, and you are not guessing at hidden constraints.

This is not a precise calculation. The practical threshold is rough: if more than half of a suit is visible, it is worth developing. If fewer than a quarter is exposed, hold off and do cross-suit work instead while more of that suit comes out.

## Tracking as Cards Are Revealed

Suit distribution changes throughout the game. Each time you flip a face-down card, you get new information. A suit you thought was mostly hidden may turn out to have several cards clustered in one column.

The key moments to update your suit count are:

**After flipping a buried card in a column you are developing.** If you reveal three Clubs in a row, that suit is becoming accessible. Consider whether a Clubs run is now worth pushing toward.

**After dealing from the stock.** Each deal adds 10 new face-up cards. Take a moment to register which suits those cards belong to. A deal that puts four Hearts on the board in one round changes the Hearts picture.

**When you complete a full run.** Removing 13 cards of one suit does not eliminate that suit, because there are two copies of each suit in the deck. But it does concentrate the remaining cards for all suits into fewer columns, which often makes distribution easier to read.

The [Spider Solitaire suit strategy guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-suit-strategy) covers how to prioritize which suit to develop first once you have a clear picture of distribution. Tracking gives you the input that decision needs.

## Cross-Suit Building When Suit Data Is Thin

Early in a 4-suit game, there is often not enough information to confidently build in-suit. Most cards are still face-down, and you do not yet know which suits are accessible.

Cross-suit building during this phase is not a mistake. It serves a purpose: clearing face-down cards, creating empty columns, and generating the information you need to decide which suits are worth developing. Each face-down card you flip is data about suit distribution that you did not have before.

The [Spider Solitaire sequence decisions guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-sequence-decisions) covers how to evaluate whether a sequence move is productive or just relocates a problem. Cross-suit builds fall into this same framework: they should accomplish something specific, like revealing a buried card or freeing a column, not just fill space.

Once the board opens and suit distribution becomes clearer, you can shift toward in-suit building for the suits that have enough cards exposed to complete.

## The 4-Suit Application

In 4-suit Spider, suit tracking is especially valuable in the first two deals. After the initial layout, you have 10 cards visible. After the first deal, you have more visible information and have revealed some face-down cards through early play.

At this point, do a rough count: how many cards of each suit can you actually see? If one suit has 7 or 8 visible cards and another has only 2, that tells you which suit is worth developing and which to treat as cross-suit material for now.

This takes about five seconds and does not need to be precise. The rough count is enough to steer your decisions for the next several moves.

Apply both together and you will find that decisions in the first two deals come with more confidence and fewer mid-game regrets.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does suit tracking work in 1-suit Spider?

No. In 1-suit Spider, every card is the same suit, so there is nothing to track. Suit tracking applies specifically to 2-suit and 4-suit games where sequence purity determines whether a run can be completed.

### How precisely do I need to count suits?

Not very. A rough sense of "most of this suit is exposed" versus "most of this suit is still hidden" is enough to steer decisions. Exact counting is not practical during play and not necessary.

### What if none of the suits have many cards exposed?

That is normal in the early game, especially after the initial deal. Focus on cross-suit building and column management until the board opens. Chasing in-suit runs before the suit is accessible wastes moves and creates sequences that are hard to dismantle later.

### Does suit tracking change the opening strategy?

It reinforces it. The core advice in the opening strategy is to avoid committing to sequences that cannot complete soon. Suit tracking gives you a concrete reason: if the suit is mostly buried, in-suit commitment is premature, and cross-suit work is the better use of early moves.

### Is 4-suit Spider harder because suits are more scattered?

Yes. With four suits across 104 cards, each suit is more spread out than in a 2-suit game. That is why suit distribution tracking is more important in 4-suit than 2-suit play, and why 4-suit requires more patience before committing to in-suit runs.
