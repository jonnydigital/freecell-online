Most people who look up Spider Solitaire end up playing the 4-suit version and losing quickly. That's partly a difficulty mismatch. Spider has several distinct versions that vary in how they play and what they demand. Understanding what separates them helps you pick the right challenge and develop skills in the right order.

## Key Takeaways

- Spider's three core versions (1-suit, 2-suit, 4-suit) share the same rules but differ dramatically in difficulty because of how often in-suit sequences can form.
- Spiderette is a shorter version played with a single deck and 7 columns. It's faster to complete but requires the same core skills.
- Scorpion shares surface similarities with Spider but plays very differently. The movement rules and dealing structure make it a distinct game.
- Most players who want to improve at 4-suit Spider should spend real time on 2-suit first. The jump from 1-suit to 4-suit skips critical intermediate skills.

## How the Three Suit Modes Differ

Standard Spider uses 104 cards, two standard 52-card decks. What changes across the three difficulty modes is how those cards are distributed among suits.

**1-suit Spider** uses only Spades. All 104 cards are the same suit. Any card of the right rank connects with any other card of that rank. In-suit sequences form constantly, and the main challenge is column management and dealing timing rather than suit matching.

**2-suit Spider** uses Spades and Hearts. A card of the same suit can stack on another of the same suit, but a red card on a black card creates a mixed-suit sequence. Mixed sequences can't be completed and removed from the board. They block columns over time.

**4-suit Spider** uses all four suits. Completing an in-suit run of 13 cards requires finding the exact right suit at each rank. Out-of-suit sequences are temporary holding positions, not progress. Every dealing decision matters more because the board fills with complexity fast.

The skill jump from 1-suit to 2-suit is real. The jump from 2-suit to 4-suit is larger. The [Spider difficulty guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-difficulty-guide) breaks down what each mode actually demands in terms of empty column management and sequencing discipline.

## Spiderette: A Shorter Spider

Spiderette uses a single 52-card deck and 7 columns instead of 10. The setup is similar to standard Spider: cards dealt face-down with the top card face-up. The stock deals to 7 columns rather than 10, and you need to complete four in-suit runs instead of eight to win.

Where Spiderette differs from standard Spider:

- Fewer columns means empty column opportunities become scarce faster
- The game resolves in 15 to 25 minutes rather than 40 to 60
- Tighter margins mean early mistakes are harder to recover from

Spiderette is useful for practice. You work through column management problems, in-suit sequencing decisions, and dealing timing in less time. The strategic core is identical to standard Spider. If you can win Spiderette at 2-suit, you have the skills to play standard Spider at 2-suit. The positions just give you less room to maneuver.

One difference to watch for: the fewer columns in Spiderette make early empty column decisions more consequential. In standard Spider, you have a wider tableau and can sometimes recover from a misplaced sequence. In Spiderette, a column filled too early often stays problematic through the rest of the game.

## Scorpion: Similar Name, Different Game

Scorpion uses one deck and 7 columns, so it superficially resembles Spiderette. The movement rules are different enough that it's a genuinely distinct game.

In Scorpion, you can move any face-up card regardless of the cards below it. In Spider and Spiderette, you can only move organized sequences. This makes certain extractions easier in Scorpion but requires tracking the board state differently.

Scorpion's goal is also four complete in-suit sequences of 13 cards each, but the path to building them is different because of what moves are available to you at any point.

The other key difference is the stock. Scorpion uses a 3-card stock (3 cards remain after the initial deal). You deal those three cards when you're stuck, and that's it. In Spider, you have more dealing opportunities. This means Scorpion ends in a stuck position more often, and recognizing when to deal that final 3 matters.

## Which Version to Play Based on Your Experience

**If you're new to Spider:** Start with 1-suit. The frequent in-suit connections let you focus on column structure and dealing timing without suit matching pressure. Most first-time players find 1-suit completable within their first several games.

**If you've won a few 1-suit games:** Move to 2-suit before jumping to 4-suit. Two suits introduce suit discipline under manageable conditions. You learn to distinguish in-suit sequences from out-of-suit ones and to plan around mixed-sequence blocks. This work doesn't happen at 1-suit, and skipping it makes 4-suit feel impossible rather than hard.

**If you can win 2-suit consistently:** 4-suit is ready for you. Your win rate will drop. That's normal. The same skills apply, but the positions that require them arise every few moves instead of occasionally.

**If you want a faster game:** Spiderette at 2-suit or 4-suit gives you the full strategic challenge in a format that fits in 20 minutes.

## What Stays Constant Across Variants

Regardless of which version you play, the core principles don't change:

- In-suit sequences are always better than mixed sequences
- Empty columns are valuable and shouldn't be filled with cards that create no forward path
- Dealing from the stock should be delayed until the tableau is ready to absorb new cards
- Face-down cards buried under long sequences are liabilities until revealed

The logic scales from 1-suit to 4-suit because the underlying principle (build sequences you can complete, not just sequences you can build) is the same at every level.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between Spider 1-suit and 4-suit?

In 1-suit, all 104 cards are the same suit, so any card of the right rank completes a sequence. In 4-suit, all four suits appear, and only a complete run in one suit can be removed. In-suit connections are much rarer, and every tableau decision has more downstream consequences.

### Is Spiderette easier or harder than standard Spider?

Spiderette at 4-suit is roughly comparable in difficulty to standard 4-suit Spider, though the shorter game gives you less room to recover from early mistakes. Spiderette at 1-suit is easier overall because the game resolves faster and suit matching isn't a factor.

### Should I play Spiderette before standard Spider?

If you're new to solitaire, yes. Spiderette gives you the same strategic problems in a shorter session. Once you're comfortable with the core mechanics, moving to the 10-column standard game opens up more complex and interesting positions.

### How is Scorpion different from Spider?

Scorpion allows you to move any face-up card, not just organized sequences. The stock is also much smaller (3 cards). These two differences make it a distinct game with its own strategic logic, even though the goal looks similar on the surface.

### Which Spider variant is most popular?

4-suit Spider is the most widely recognized version, largely because it was the default in Windows Solitaire. 1-suit gets played more by beginners. 2-suit has a strong following among players who want real challenge without the extreme difficulty of 4-suit.
