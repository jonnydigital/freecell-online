Spider Solitaire has a low win rate. Even in 1-suit mode, losing is common. In 4-suit mode, most games end without a completed board. The question is not whether you will face losing positions. It is how to tell a genuinely lost game from one you gave up too soon.

Restarting at the right moment is not quitting. Restarting at the wrong moment is practice that never teaches you anything.

## Key Takeaways

- Most positions that feel unwinnable are not. Run a full check before deciding.
- A game is genuinely lost when no empty columns remain, no suit runs can be completed, and the stock is exhausted.
- Restarting too early builds bad habits. Staying too long in a dead game wastes time.
- The [Spider cheat sheet](/spider-cheat-sheet) gives a quick priority reference when you feel stuck. Try it before restarting.
- Every restart is a data point. If you are restarting more than finishing, the problem is usually an earlier decision, not bad luck.

## What Makes a Spider Game Genuinely Lost

A Spider game is genuinely unwinnable when all three of the following are true at the same time:

No empty columns remain and none can be created. Without empty columns, there is no way to reroute sequences, combine partial runs, or free buried cards. The board has locked itself.

No complete suit run can be formed, even with all remaining deals. If no suit has a path to completion, 52 cards will never leave the board.

The stock is exhausted. With no new cards coming, the only resource left is the existing tableau. If that resource is locked, the game is over.

When all three are true simultaneously, restarting is correct. When only one or two are true, the game is usually still alive.

## Signs You Are Stuck, Not Beaten

Stuck feels like lost but is different. Stuck means your current scan of the board is not finding useful moves. Lost means no useful moves exist regardless of how hard you look.

Before restarting, check for these:

**A short sequence you have not moved.** Two or three cards in a column may be movable onto a receiving card in another column without any visible gain. Sometimes that move frees a card one step down that unlocks a whole chain.

**A partial suit run you have not noticed.** A 7 of Spades in one column and an 8-7 of Spades somewhere else is not a complete run, but it is combinable. Combining it may create room for another suit move you missed.

**An empty column hidden by a long sequence.** It sounds obvious, but players sometimes lose track of which columns are truly empty versus which ones are short. Count again.

**A deal you are treating as your last option instead of your best option.** Dealing is not giving up forward progress. Some positions genuinely need new cards to unblock a suit. The [Spider Solitaire when-to-deal guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-when-to-deal) covers exactly when dealing is the right choice.

If you have checked all of these and still find nothing, then the position is worth evaluating more carefully for a restart.

## How to Assess Whether the Position Is Recoverable

A fast assessment runs like this:

Count empty columns. If you have two or more empty columns, the position has significant flexibility. Continue.

Look for the suit closest to completion. Any suit with six or more cards in a connected run is a near-term target. If at least one suit has a clear path, that path is worth following before giving up.

Check whether the longest run on the board can be extended. A 10-card Hearts run with an 11th Hearts card buried two positions away is worth fighting for. A situation where every suit run is blocked by a card of a different suit, with no empty column to route around it, is a harder case.

The [Spider Solitaire dead positions guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-dead-positions) covers the specific tableau patterns that cannot be resolved regardless of play. If your position matches one of those patterns, restarting is the right call.

## Why Restarting Too Often Hurts Your Game

Restarting a difficult position before fully exploring it is one of the most common habits that keeps players from improving.

The reason is that every genuinely hard position is a learning opportunity. Getting yourself out of a tight spot, even with undo and experimentation, builds pattern recognition. You start to see which earlier move created the constraint. You understand what the board looks like when it has two empty columns versus one. You build judgment.

A player who restarts at the first sign of difficulty never faces those moments. They stay at the same skill level because they never learn what losing positions actually look like before they become irreversible.

The [Spider Solitaire how-to-improve guide](/blog/spider-solitaire-how-to-improve) makes this point in a different context: players improve most when they play through difficulty, not around it.

## Why Staying Too Long Also Costs You

The other failure mode is grinding through positions that are mathematically dead because giving up feels like admitting defeat.

This costs time and builds false confidence. A player who finishes every game without regard for whether it was winnable will sometimes "win" by getting lucky on a deal while in a poor position. That outcome does not teach good play. It reinforces luck-based thinking instead of skill-based thinking.

If you have checked all columns, tried every sequence combination, evaluated whether dealing helps, and confirmed that no empty columns can be created, the game is done. Restarting now is rational, not emotional.

## The Single Question That Guides the Decision

When you are unsure, ask: can I name a sequence of moves that creates at least one empty column or completes at least one suit run?

If yes, play those moves. If no, check the stock. If the stock is also gone, the position is lost.

This question cuts through the feeling of being stuck and forces a concrete evaluation. Either you find a path and play it, or you confirm there is none. Both outcomes are useful.

You can [play Spider Solitaire with undo](/spider) and use this check in real time. Undo is valuable here: when you think you're stuck, try a sequence forward, then undo if it leads nowhere. The goal is to explore the position, not to commit blindly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if a Spider Solitaire game is impossible before I start?

Standard Spider deals are shuffled randomly, so there is no way to know in advance whether a specific game is winnable without solving it. The spider-winnability page has overall win-rate context. At a tactical level, the first few deals often reveal whether the board has the structural conditions for a win.

### Is it better to use undo or just restart?

Undo is better if you can trace the specific move that created the problem. Restarting is better when the position has been poor for many moves and the root issue is not recoverable from. If you use undo more than five or six times without finding a better branch, that is a reasonable signal that the game needs a restart rather than a correction.

### Does restarting count as a loss in my stats?

That depends on the app you are using. In most Spider Solitaire implementations, resigning or restarting a game counts as a loss. If win percentage matters to you, that is a reason to work through difficult positions rather than restart early. For most players focused on improvement, the stats matter less than the practice.

### How often should a good Spider Solitaire player restart?

A strong player restarts occasionally but rarely in 1-suit mode and sometimes in 4-suit mode. 4-suit Spider has a low win rate even for experienced players, so more restarts are expected. If you are restarting most games in 1-suit mode, the issue is earlier play decisions, not bad luck.

### What is the fastest way to know if a game is still winnable?

Count empty columns and check whether any single suit has a continuous run of six or more cards. If you have at least one empty column and one suit with six consecutive in-suit cards, the game almost certainly still has a path forward. If you have no empty columns and no suit run longer than four, the position is under serious pressure and worth assessing carefully.
